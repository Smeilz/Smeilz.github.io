Living Matrix

Wine and specialty coffee have a shared vocabulary for quality. Medicinal plants still do not. Living Matrix gives every plant two passports that describe the same thing in two languages:

Traditional passport

Taste, temperature, channels it enters and organs it affects, as described in Chinese medicine and Ayurveda.

Molecular passport

Lead compounds, target genes, pathways, and what can be seen in lab tests.

Personal response

What the plant does in one body: sleep, heart-rate variability, season. N = 1.

The idea underneath: taste is data. Bitter often points to polyphenols, pungent to volatile terpenes, astringent to tannins. Old herbalists ran a sensory assay without knowing the molecules. Now the two can be matched.

Example: chrysanthemum + black goji

Chrysanthemum morifolium + Lycium ruthenicumClassic pair: “clear Liver heat, brighten the eyes”
Traditional passport
  • Chrysanthemum (Jú Huā): sweet, bitter, slightly cold; enters the Lung and Liver channels
  • Used together to clear heat and support the eyes
Molecular passport
  • Black goji: anthocyanins, mostly petunidin glycosides; linked to lower AGEs, calmer RAGE signalling and gut microbiota changes (Wang 2018; Jin 2015)
  • Chrysanthemum: luteolin and apigenin, studied for retinal microcirculation and vasodilation
Reading
“Clearing Liver heat” here reads as anti-inflammatory plus endothelial support. Two languages, one territory.

How I label evidence

Database links between a plant and a gene are often lab-dish results, not clinical facts. I mark every claim:

★★★Human clinical studies
★★Animal studies
★In vitro or computational prediction
